    Is Consciousness an Integral Part of the Universe?

    A product of Cosmic Corner e-magazine.

    For the past ten years or so, I have been fascinated by the possible connection between consciousness and the physical Universe. The scientific literature contains a growing number of papers on the subject, particularly from neuroscientists, along with contributions from physicists, cosmologists and science writers like myself, exploring this fascinating possibility.

    What exactly are scientists looking for?

    We know that consciousness is closely associated with brain function. In humans and other mammals, the cerebral cortex is generally regarded as the primary seat of conscious awareness, while the reticular formation of the midbrain and certain nuclei of the thalamus appear to regulate or “gate” the flow of information between different regions of the cortex. Together, these structures enable our conscious experience.

    Some researchers, however, suggest that consciousness may extend beyond conventional neuroscience. They propose that, at the quantum level, consciousness could somehow be linked to the fundamental fabric of the Universe. Two key concepts often arise in these discussions: quantum mechanics and quantum entanglement.

    Several examples are frequently cited in support of this possibility.

    One recent example comes from researchers at Wellesley College in Massachusetts. In 2026, they reported evidence supporting the hypothesis that quantum processes occurring within tiny structures inside brain cells may contribute to consciousness. In experiments involving anaesthetized rats, they suggested that microscopic structures known as microtubules may play a significant role in conscious experience. While these findings are intriguing, they remain an active area of research rather than established scientific fact but this is how science starts exploring theory.

    Another example concerns one of the stranger features of quantum mechanics. Before a particle is measured, it can exist in a superposition, meaning it is described by several possible states simultaneously. Once a measurement is made, the wavefunction appears to collapse into a single outcome. This has led some people to wonder whether consciousness itself plays a role in the measurement process. However, most physicists explain wavefunction collapse through quantum interactions with measuring devices or the surrounding environment rather than requiring a conscious observer. The question remains one of the great philosophical debates in modern quantum physics.

    Perhaps the best-known theory linking consciousness and quantum physics is the Orchestrated Objective Reduction (Orch-OR) model developed by Sir Roger Penrose and Dr Stuart Hameroff during the 1990s & later in 2013. Their theory proposes that quantum processes occurring within neuronal microtubules give rise to conscious awareness. Although highly influential and widely discussed, the theory remains controversial, with many neuroscientists arguing that the warm, noisy environment of the brain is unlikely to sustain delicate quantum states for long enough to influence thought.

    Another thought-provoking contribution comes from microbiologist Peter Noble. In 2024, he suggested that some cells may retain organized activity even after the death of the organism and argued that life, death and cellular behavior may be more complex than previously understood. Some researchers have also proposed that multicellular organisms should be viewed as cooperative communities of human cells and microorganisms. While these ideas broaden our understanding of life, they do not yet demonstrate that individual cells possess consciousness. Does this explain why hair and fingernails continue to grow after clinical death has passed?

    These examples span neuroscience, quantum physics and biology. But an even larger question remains.

    Could consciousness exist on a cosmic scale?

    Some researchers have explored whether the large-scale structure of the Universe shares similarities with the organization of the human brain. Three concepts commonly appear in these discussions: self-organization, information flow and fractals.

    1.0 Self-Organization: - In their paper The Quantitative Comparison Between the Neural Network and the Cosmic Web, Franco Vazza and Alberto Feletti compared the structure of the Universe’s cosmic web with the network of neurons inside the human brain. For some time, this has stirred curiosity among astronomers and astrophysicists alike. Despite the enormous difference in scale, both systems consist of interconnected filaments joined at dense nodes, forming remarkably similar network patterns.

    The authors do not claim that the Universe is literally a brain. Rather, they suggest that both systems may obey similar mathematical principles governing the organization of complex networks. Whether this similarity reflects deep physical laws or is simply a coincidence remains an open question.

    2.0 Information Flow: Another intriguing idea concerns the way information moves through complex systems. Biophysicist and mathematician Douglas Youvan has proposed that intelligence may not simply emerge from brains but could instead represent a fundamental property of information itself. Drawing upon decades of research in genetics, biology and information theory, he suggests that certain complex structures—whether biological or cosmic—may be capable of processing information in ways that resemble intelligence.

    At present, this remains an interesting hypothesis rather than an accepted scientific theory.

    3.0 Fractals: Fractals are patterns that repeat across many different scales.

    At the smallest scales we find atoms, subatomic particles, quantum fields and virtual particles. At the largest scales we observe stars, galaxies, galaxy clusters and the immense cosmic web. Some researchers have suggested that the Universe may possess a fractal-like organization, where similar patterns emerge regardless of scale.

    Whether the Universe is truly fractal remains a subject of ongoing research, but the recurring appearance of self-similar structures is certainly fascinating.

    A Philosophical Question to wind up:

    Taken together, these ideas encourage us to think differently about the Universe. We see nature organizing itself into atoms, molecules, stars, galaxies and living organisms. Gravity gathers enormous clouds of gas into stars, while entropy continually drives systems toward greater disorder, even as local regions become more organized.

    Is this simply the natural outcome of the laws of physics operating over billions of years? Or does the remarkable organization we observe hint at something deeper—perhaps an underlying informational principle, or even a form of universal consciousness that we are unable to describe?

    At present, science has no definitive answer. Most physicists and neuroscientists regard consciousness as an emergent property of biological brains rather than a property of the Universe itself. Nevertheless, the search continues, and the intersection of cosmology, quantum physics, neuroscience and philosophy remains one of the most exciting frontiers in modern science.

    Whether the Universe is merely governed by elegant physical laws or possesses a deeper form of awareness is one of the greatest unanswered questions. My hunch is, there is something going on out there where unification of consciousness and the big beautiful universe is keeping a secret from us.
